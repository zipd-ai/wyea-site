Specialty insurance

Reading policies with the endorsements that amend them

By WYEA · Published September 27, 2026 · Updated September 27, 2026

To know what a policy says today, read the base wording together with every endorsement that amends it, in order of effective date, and take the latest agreed text of each clause. An answer drawn from the base wording alone will be wrong for any clause that was changed mid-term.

What endorsements change

An endorsement records a change to an issued policy. In specialty lines the changes are routine. An additional insured is added, a limit is increased for one location, a notice period is shortened, or an exclusion is deleted by agreement with the broker. By the end of its term, a cyber or D&O policy can carry several endorsements.

Endorsements are also written in different ways, and each kind has to be read against the policy in its own way.

  • Amending one clause by number. An endorsement that amends the notice period in Condition 11 to forty-five days changes one figure and leaves the rest of the condition standing.
  • Deleting and replacing a section. A new business interruption section replaces the old one whole, including its waiting period and its exclusions.
  • Changing a definition. A new definition of Claim changes every clause that uses the word, even though none of those clauses is mentioned.
  • Amending an earlier endorsement. The order of effective dates then decides what the policy says.

What market guidance says about changes

The London market's Contract Certainty Code of Practice states that "Contract changes need to be certain and documented promptly." Its guidance adds that changes are made by documented mutual agreement, "clearly identifying the effective date of the change."

The effective date is what lets anyone reconstruct the wording in force on a given day, and it is also where mistakes creep in. An endorsement issued in March may take effect from January. Two endorsements may amend the same condition, and the reader has to work out which one stands. A question about a claim notified in February needs the wording in force at that date, which may differ from what the full file says today.

How the reading is usually done by hand

A wordings technician asked about one clause on one policy pulls the file, lists the endorsements, sorts them by effective date and reads each one for the clause in question. For one policy that is manageable.

A question across a book is harder. Asking which in-force cyber policies have a waiting period under twelve hours means doing the same reading for every policy. Endorsements are often stored apart from the policy they amend, in a separate folder or as an email attachment, and some arrive as scans. At renewal the question comes back: if terms carry forward "as expiring", someone has to decide which mid-term endorsements the renewal wording picks up.

How the engine reads a policy with its endorsements

The engine works out which endorsement amends which policy and keeps that link, along with which deal each document belongs to and who the parties are. It does this once, and the link is there for every later question.

When you ask about a clause, the answer reflects the wording in force. It quotes the endorsement that changed the clause, with its effective date, next to the base clause it amends. Each line opens the document it came from.

The insureds, clauses and dates in this example are invented. No client material appears anywhere on this site.

A passage has to be found in one of your documents before it is written as an answer. If it cannot be found, it is flagged. A scanned endorsement with no readable text goes to a person unanswered, because an unread endorsement could change the result.

An answer points at the documents as they read that day, so a later edit to a file does not change what was relied on. Copies of the pages an answer relied on are kept past your storage provider's file history. Every answer records who asked, who approved it, and when.

The engine finds and quotes the wording in force. Whether a loss is covered under that wording is a decision for your people, and the engine makes no claims or underwriting decisions.

When a shared product is the better fit

If your policies are standard forms with endorsements chosen from a fixed list, your policy administration system probably already records which endorsements attach to each policy. A shared product will usually serve you better, and we will say so on the first call. The engine is built for books where endorsements are negotiated and manuscript, and are kept as files in your document system.
